## 4.5 Coupling and Predictive Content of Stablecoin and Crypto Systemic Risk

This section examines whether systemic risk in stablecoins is simply a manifestation of broader cryptocurrency-market stress. The comparison does not use the numerical equality of the two FRM indices because their levels depend on different asset universes and penalty scales. Instead, it evaluates three related margins: co-occurrence of high-risk states, directional out-of-sample predictive content, and event-specific differences in response. The primary Stable FRM is the reference-adjusted, eleven-coin screened series developed in Section 4.4. The primary Crypto FRM is independently re-estimated from the fifteen assets specified in the presentation, using the same 90-day rolling quantile-Lasso algorithm, a 5% lower-tail quantile and 25 path steps. Assets enter the latter system when they are active within the estimation window; no missing observations are interpolated.

The two indices exhibit substantial state-level coupling but little synchronization in daily innovations (Tables 4.5a–c). The correlation between their log levels is 0.568, whereas the correlation between daily log changes is −0.022. Defining a high-risk state as an observation at or above the index-specific full-sample 80th percentile, each series records 439 high-risk days and 268 of these days are shared. The probability that Stable FRM is high conditional on a high Crypto FRM is 61.0%, compared with 9.7% when Crypto FRM is below its threshold. The resulting odds ratio is 14.49 (90-day moving-block bootstrap 95% interval, 3.89–55.15). These thresholds are retrospective descriptive devices rather than real-time signals. The evidence therefore indicates that the two systems frequently occupy the same persistent risk regime, but does not imply that their day-to-day risk innovations are identical.

Predictive content is assessed through a symmetric forecast design. For each horizon \(h\), the dependent variable is regressed on the current log Stable FRM, the current log Crypto FRM, or both indices jointly. The four horizons follow the presentation: 10 days, 5 weeks (35 days), 3 months (90 days) and 5 months (150 days). Stablecoin stress is measured by the log of one plus the mean future reference-adjusted downside peg deviation; future one-percent downside-depeg breadth is retained as a supplementary outcome. Broad-crypto stress is measured by the log future annualised volatility of a daily crypto return weighted by lagged market capitalisation. At forecast origin \(t\), a historical origin \(s\) enters the training sample only when its complete outcome window has already been observed, such that \(s+h\leq t\). This embargo prevents overlapping long-horizon targets from entering the model before their outcomes are known. Forecasts use both expanding estimation and a pre-specified rolling window of 63 fully observed targets. Campbell–Thompson out-of-sample \(R^2\) is computed relative to the historical-mean forecast. Nested comparisons use one-sided Clark–West tests, non-nested Stable-versus-Crypto comparisons use HAC loss-difference tests, and p values are adjusted across the four horizons using the Holm procedure.

Under expanding estimation, Crypto FRM provides the strongest forecast of future broad-crypto volatility (Fig. 4.5 and Tables 4.5d,e). Its out-of-sample \(R^2\) is 0.213, 0.269, 0.294 and 0.174 at the 10-, 35-, 90- and 150-day horizons, respectively. The Clark–West comparison with the historical mean remains significant at all four horizons after Holm adjustment. Adding Stable FRM produces joint-model \(R^2\) values of 0.190, 0.175, 0.247 and 0.127, but the joint model does not outperform Crypto FRM alone. The strict minimum-GACV specification yields almost identical estimates. In the balanced nine-asset Crypto system, which removes variation in asset availability, Crypto FRM retains positive out-of-sample \(R^2\) values of 0.178, 0.160, 0.201 and 0.089. The conclusion that Crypto FRM contains information about subsequent broad-market volatility is therefore not driven by the numerical screening rule or by the changing fifteen-asset coverage.

The cross-market forecast of stablecoin downside pressure is weaker and definition-sensitive (Fig. 4.5). In the dynamic fifteen-asset system, Crypto FRM produces expanding-window out-of-sample \(R^2\) values of 0.096, 0.070, 0.050 and 0.095. Only the 10-day comparison with the historical mean remains significant after correction across the four horizons. Stable FRM alone produces corresponding values of 0.008, approximately zero, 0.013 and 0.051, and adding it to Crypto FRM does not improve on the Crypto-only model. More importantly, the Crypto-only values fall to 0.007, 0.006, 0.003 and 0.035 in the balanced nine-asset system. Thus, the data do not support a definition-invariant claim that broad-crypto FRM predicts subsequent stablecoin depegging pressure.

Forecast performance also depends on the estimation history. In the rolling-63 specification, only one of the 24 combinations formed by the two primary outcomes, four horizons and three predictor models has a positive out-of-sample \(R^2\). The contrast with the expanding estimates indicates parameter instability rather than a time-invariant forecasting relation. This negative result is economically consistent with the episodic character of stablecoin stress: a short window can be dominated by one event regime and need not preserve the longer-run relation between systemic-risk states.

The paired event design further separates common market stress from shock-specific transmission (Table 4.5f). It uses the same event dates, stages, year effects, linear trend and Newey–West HAC(90) inference as Section 4.4. In addition to estimating each FRM separately, the model is applied directly to \(\log(FRM^{Stable}_t/FRM^{Crypto}_t)\). This ratio specification tests the difference in event response while retaining the contemporaneous covariance between the two indices. Over days 0–30, Crypto FRM responds more strongly to KuCoin, the May 2021 market shock and Terra: the Stable-minus-Crypto response is −49.8%, −31.7% and −34.2%, respectively, with all three differences significant after Holm adjustment across the six events. Conversely, the difference is +13.7% during FTX and +27.5% during the SVB-linked USDC depeg. These estimates identify intermediary-liquidity and reserve-custody shocks as episodes in which stablecoin-network stress is disproportionately large relative to broad-crypto stress.

The Iran–Hormuz episode does not display such stablecoin-specific amplification. Over days 0–30, Stable FRM falls by 24.5% and Crypto FRM falls by 17.6%; the direct difference is −8.3% and is not statistically distinguishable from zero after Holm adjustment. Hence, the external geopolitical shock does not generate a broad new stablecoin risk state in this sample, despite its effects on oil and conventional risk markets documented in Section 4.4. The local Crypto data end on 6 April 2026, so the event's 0–30-day window is complete but its 31–60-day persistence window is not. The latter is therefore not used for inference.

Taken together, the results qualify rather than reject the presentation's distinction between the two systems. Stablecoin and broad-crypto risk share persistent high-risk regimes, but their daily innovations, forecast content and event sensitivities are not interchangeable. Crypto FRM is a robust indicator of subsequent broad-market volatility; Stable FRM captures additional shock-specific information most clearly during FTX and SVB, but it is not a universal leading indicator. The evidence supports a mechanism-dependent interpretation of stablecoins as normal-time anchors that can become risk amplifiers when confidence in intermediation, reserves or redemption is directly impaired.
